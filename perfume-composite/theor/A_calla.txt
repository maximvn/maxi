TRANSPARENT GLASS, PRINTED MARK — TWO RULES THAT BOTH HOLD.
First, THE GLASS IS TRANSPARENT. This is real emerald glass, not painted plastic. You look through the bottle and out the other side: whatever stands behind it is visible through the body, tinted deep green, laterally compressed and vertically flipped by the curve, and broken along the facet edges into slightly offset panels — each facet bending the background a little differently. The green runs light and luminous where the glass is thin near the neck and edges, and dense, saturated, almost black-green through the deepest part of the body and the thick base. The thick base acts as a lens and throws a green caustic onto whatever the bottle stands on.
Second, THE PRINTED MARK IS NOT TRANSPARENT. The monogram and the words BRUME ORPIN and THEOR are pale opaque ink lying on the outer surface of the glass. You do not see the background through the letterforms. They sit flat on the curve, following it, and they do not refract, distort or invert with what is behind the bottle. They stay the same pale tone regardless of what passes behind them.

WHAT THE GLASS REFLECTS — THE HALF THAT IS USUALLY MISSING.
Glass transmits what is behind it and mirrors what is in front of it at the same time. A render that only transmits looks fake. The front surface of the cone must carry mirrored highlights of the scene's own light source, stretched and bent by the taper, with the darker parts of the room reading as deep vertical bands down the flanks. The facet edges catch thin bright specular lines. The white ribbed cap picks up the light in the grooves of its fluting so the ribs read as alternating light and shade, and the gold collar returns a warm metallic band with a bright top edge and a darker underside.

PHYSICAL EVIDENCE — THE IMPERFECTIONS THAT PROVE IT IS REAL.
A flawless object reads as computer-generated. Include, subtly: a speck or two of dust catching the light, a faint barely-there fingerprint low on the glass, a hair-fine mould seam or one tiny bubble deep in the thick base, the smallest wear on a cap edge. Nothing dirty, nothing distracting — just evidence of a physical object that has been handled and set down.

THE PICTURE — READ THIS TWICE.
The bottle is NOT standing. It is lying on its side, and the stone is carrying it.
The bottle lies almost horizontal, its long axis running across the frame at roughly seventy degrees off vertical, the white capped end raised a little higher than the base. Its left flank rests along the upper shoulder of the chalk-white boulder, the stone bearing the bottle's weight the way a rock cradles something set down on it. Contact is along a length of the glass, not a single point: the flank of the cone settles into the stone's surface over a span of several centimetres, with a tight dark line of occlusion running the whole way along that seam and a soft green bounce thrown from the glass onto the chalky white right beside it.
Critically: the bottle touches NOTHING ELSE. No floor, no ground plane, no table, no base contact, no shadow of the bottle falling on any surface beneath it. The stone is its only support. If the bottle's base appears to be resting on a floor, the shot has failed — it is the stone that holds it, not the ground.
THE FRAME IS TILTED. The whole photograph is rotated counter-clockwise, camera canted to the left by roughly twenty-five degrees, and it is that cant which reveals the lean: everything in the picture tips with it, the stone included, so gravity reads on a diagonal. Verticals are deliberately not vertical. This is a Dutch tilt, committed to fully, not a slight accident of level.
The calla lily arcs in from the opposite direction, its stem crossing the bottle's diagonal so the two lines make an X somewhere in the upper third, the flower head tipping down toward the glass without touching it. Crop closer than the reference: the stone fills more of the frame, the bottle runs boldly across it, and one clean pocket of empty grey is left for breathing room. Off-centre, unbalanced on purpose, alive rather than arranged.

LIGHT.
One broad soft source from the front-left and slightly above, the same quality that rakes the stone's texture in @image2 — but push it harder than the reference: let the far side of the boulder fall into genuine shadow so the form turns, and let the grey ground go darker as it recedes. The bottle takes that light along its upturned flank as a long soft highlight running the length of the cone, with the underside in deep green shadow and one bright specular tracing a facet edge. Where glass meets stone the light is pinched out entirely — that dark seam is what sells the weight. No new light source, no rim light the plate does not have, no sunlight.

COLOUR.
Cool neutral grey, chalk white clay, oxblood flower, emerald glass. The green is the only saturated cool note and it must sing against the wine red without either shifting hue. White balance neutral, around 5400K. Low contrast but not flat, blacks soft and open, long gentle rolloff. Muted, quiet, gallery-like.

This was photographed on a table, not built in software. Fine luminance-only grain around two percent.
