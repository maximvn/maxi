TRANSPARENT GLASS, PRINTED MARK — TWO RULES THAT BOTH HOLD.
First, THE GLASS IS TRANSPARENT. This is real emerald glass, not painted plastic. You look through the bottle and out the other side: whatever stands behind it is visible through the body, tinted deep green, laterally compressed and vertically flipped by the curve, and broken along the facet edges into slightly offset panels — each facet bending the background a little differently. The green runs light and luminous where the glass is thin near the neck and edges, and dense, saturated, almost black-green through the deepest part of the body and the thick base. The thick base acts as a lens and throws a green caustic onto whatever the bottle stands on.
Second, THE PRINTED MARK IS NOT TRANSPARENT. The monogram and the words BRUME ORPIN and THEOR are pale opaque ink lying on the outer surface of the glass. You do not see the background through the letterforms. They sit flat on the curve, following it, and they do not refract, distort or invert with what is behind the bottle. They stay the same pale tone regardless of what passes behind them.

WHAT THE GLASS REFLECTS — THE HALF THAT IS USUALLY MISSING.
Glass transmits what is behind it and mirrors what is in front of it at the same time. A render that only transmits looks fake. The front surface of the cone must carry mirrored highlights of the scene's own light source, stretched and bent by the taper, with the darker parts of the room reading as deep vertical bands down the flanks. The facet edges catch thin bright specular lines. The white ribbed cap picks up the light in the grooves of its fluting so the ribs read as alternating light and shade, and the gold collar returns a warm metallic band with a bright top edge and a darker underside.

PHYSICAL EVIDENCE — THE IMPERFECTIONS THAT PROVE IT IS REAL.
A flawless object reads as computer-generated. Include, subtly: a speck or two of dust catching the light, a faint barely-there fingerprint low on the glass, a hair-fine mould seam or one tiny bubble deep in the thick base, the smallest wear on a cap edge. Nothing dirty, nothing distracting — just evidence of a physical object that has been handled and set down.

THE PICTURE.
Build this as a sensual, half-hidden still life, not a centred product record.
Set the charpai off-centre, turned maybe thirty degrees so you see it three-quarter rather than square-on, one pair of legs nearer the lens than the other. Stand the THEOR bottle upright on the woven jute, not dead centre but pushed toward one side of the panel, base flat and fully in contact so the rope compresses slightly under its weight. Size it a little taller than the legs are high.
A SHEER VEIL. A length of translucent gauze — silk organza or fine voile, warm ivory going amber where the light passes through it — falls from above out of frame and drifts diagonally across the picture, caught in slow movement. It passes IN FRONT of part of the bottle: the lower body and one flank read through the cloth, softened, desaturated, the green glowing dully through the weave, while the shoulder, the gold collar and the white cap emerge clear and sharp above and beside it. That partial concealment is the whole idea — the eye completes what the fabric hides. Where the veil crosses the hard key it goes luminous and you can see the individual threads; where it falls into shadow it disappears into the brown. One edge of the cloth lifts and curls, slightly motion-blurred from a long exposure, so the fabric moves while the glass stays perfectly still.
THE REEDS. Not one stalk but three or four dried wheat and reed stems of different heights, entering from the lower left at different angles, leaning in loose disorder. One passes BEHIND the bottle — and where it crosses behind the body it must be seen THROUGH the glass, tinted green, thickened and displaced sideways by the taper of the cone and broken into offset segments at the facet edges. Above the shoulder that same stem continues unrefracted at its true position, and the offset between the two must be visible. That is the detail that proves the glass is real. Another stalk leans against the near leg of the charpai; a third lies fallen across the floor in the foreground, catching a sliver of light. Awns and fine hairs on the ears catch the key as bright filaments.

LIGHT.
One hard-edged directional source from the upper left, close in, harder and more raking than the reference. It cuts a defined shaft across the set, ignites the veil, edges the rope weave, and throws long clearly-shaped shadows of the charpai legs, the reeds and the bottle down and to the right. Everything outside that shaft falls away fast into near-black. Key to fill roughly eight to one — let the shadow side go genuinely dark and let large areas of the frame hold almost nothing.
On the bottle: a hard bright specular down the left facet edge, the white ribbed cap catching the light in its grooves so the fluting reads as crisp alternating light and dark, the gold collar flashing a hot line along its upper edge. The green body glows where the light passes through and drops to near-black on the unlit flank. A green transmitted pool falls on the jute beside the base. Tight dark occlusion where glass meets rope, no gap between base and shadow — if they do not meet, the shot has failed.
